## Overview

The Cactus Cat is a creature of American 'fearsome critters' folklore — the tall-tale bestiary developed by 19th- and early-20th-century lumberjacks, ranchers, and outdoor workers and partly canonised in print by William T. Cox's 'Fearsome Creatures of the Lumberwoods, with a Few Desert and Mountain Beasts' (1910) and Henry H. Tryon's 'Fearsome Critters' (1939). The Cactus Cat is described as a bobcat-sized animal whose body is covered in stiff, hair-like thorns, with especially long spines projecting from the legs and a distinctive branched, thorn-tipped tail. According to the tradition, the cat prowls the deserts of Arizona, New Mexico, and Texas at night, using its bony, blade-edged forelegs to slash open barrel cacti and saguaros. The exuded sap ferments in the desert sun over several days, and on a return circuit the Cactus Cat drinks the alcoholic juice and becomes thoroughly drunk, producing the strange yowling, screaming, and barking that echo through canyons after dark. The figure is unambiguously a tall tale: in lumberjack and ranching culture, fearsome-critter stories functioned as humour, hazing of greenhorn newcomers, and explanatory folklore for the strange noises of unfamiliar wildernesses. The Cactus Cat occupies the desert equivalent of the Hodag, the Squonk, the Hoop Snake, and Cox's other fearsome critters.

## Theories & Explanations

### Skeptical

**Occupational tall tale** _(American folklorists, Daniel Cohen)_

The Cactus Cat is a deliberately humorous tall tale invented by lumberjacks and ranchers, often used to haze 'greenhorn' newcomers who would be sent out to find or trap one.

**Explanatory folklore for desert nighttime sounds** _(Folklore-studies analysis)_

Strange nocturnal sounds in the desert (wind through saguaros, coyotes, foxes, and nightjars) provide the imaginative material for the 'drunken yowling' attribute.
